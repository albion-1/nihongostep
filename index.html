Before Unit 1 · optional orientation

Introduction to Japanese

If Japanese is completely new to you, this page explains what you are looking at before the first real lesson. There is no test, and nothing here is a progression requirement.

The big picture: Japanese has its own writing systems, its usual sentence order differs from English, and small words called particles help show what each part of a sentence is doing. You will learn all of this gradually.
1

Japanese uses three writing systems

Normal Japanese writing mixes hiragana, katakana, and kanji. You do not choose one system and write everything with it.

あ か し

Hiragana

A phonetic script: the characters mainly represent sounds. Hiragana is used constantly for grammar and many ordinary words.

か = ka
ア カ シ

Katakana

Another phonetic script representing the same basic sounds. It is commonly used for foreign words, foreign names, sound effects, and emphasis.

カ = ka
日 本 人

Kanji

Meaning-bearing characters used inside normal Japanese words. A kanji may have different readings depending on the word it appears in.

日本 = Japan
Kana is the collective name for hiragana + katakana. So when Nihongo Step says “kana,” it means those phonetic characters.
2

Romaji is a temporary reading aid

Romaji means writing Japanese sounds with the Latin alphabet—the letters used in English. For example:

みず → mizu → water

Nihongo Step uses romaji at the beginning so you are not forced to learn grammar and a new alphabet at exactly the same moment. It is scaffolding, not the final goal. As you prove that you know kana, the course removes that help.

3

English is commonly SVO. Japanese is commonly SOV.

These abbreviations describe the usual order of three basic sentence parts:

S = Subject — who or what performs/is responsible for the action V = Verb — the action or state O = Object — the thing directly affected by the action
English · SVO
SIsubject
Vdrinkverb
Owaterobject
Subject → Verb → Object
Japanese · commonly SOV
Sわたし はwatashi wa · I / as for me
Oみず をmizu o · water
Vのみますnomimasu · drink
Subject/topic → Object → Verb
Do not turn this into a rigid translation trick. Japanese is not simply “English with the verb moved to the end.” Subjects are often omitted when they are obvious, topic and subject are not always the same thing, and particle-marked chunks can sometimes move around. The important beginner habit is: expect the main verb toward the end.
4

Particles label relationships

Japanese uses short grammatical markers called particles. They usually come immediately after the word or phrase whose job they are marking.

わたしwatashi
I
はwa
topic
みずmizu
water
をo
object
のみますnomimasu
drink

は (wa) tells us what the sentence is about. を (o) marks water as the thing affected by “drink.” Unit 1 will teach these properly; you do not need to memorize them from this page.

One unusual detail: some particles have special pronunciations. The particle は is written ha but pronounced wa; を is normally pronounced o; and later you will meet へ, pronounced e as a particle.
5

A few grammar words are worth knowing

You do not need a linguistics background. These are simply names for things the course will point out:

SubjectThe person or thing associated with the verb. Japanese often leaves it unstated when context makes it obvious.
ObjectThe person or thing directly affected by an action, such as “water” in “I drink water.”
VerbAn action or state, such as go, drink, read, or exist.
ParticleA short marker after a word or phrase that helps show its relationship to the sentence.
TenseGrammar that helps place something in time, such as past versus non-past.
ConjugationChanging a word's form to express things such as past, negative, politeness, or other meanings.
6

Japanese often leaves obvious information unstated

English usually makes us say things like “I drink water.” Japanese can omit “I” when everyone already knows who is being discussed.

Full beginner-friendly version わたし は みず を のみます。 watashi wa mizu o nomimasu · I drink water.
→
When “I” is obvious みず を のみます。 mizu o nomimasu · [I] drink water.

This is one reason Japanese can feel very different from English. Nihongo Step will initially use fuller sentences so you can see the grammar clearly, then gradually expose you to more natural omission.

7

You are not expected to learn everything at once

✓ You do not need to memorize hundreds of kanji before making sentences.
✓ You do not need to understand every grammar term before beginning.
✓ You do not need to master kana before seeing your first sentence.
✓ If you already know something, mastery practice lets you prove it instead of relearning it.
✓ Romaji support is temporary and can disappear as kana knowledge is proven or deliberately waived.
✓ Later units will add kanji, more natural grammar, listening, and eventually conversation skills.
8

About & settings

This beta now covers four complete beginner units while keeping progression centered on real sentence use rather than XP or streaks.

What is in v0.4

Introduction: optional zero-knowledge orientation covering the writing systems, romaji, SVO/SOV word order, particles, and basic grammar vocabulary.

Unit 1: first Japanese sentences, basic verb forms, existence, adjectives, wants, and the first ten hiragana.

Unit 2: a full core-particle unit covering は, が, を, に, で, へ, と, の, も, から, and まで.

Unit 3: the polite verb system, negative/past/past-negative forms, verb families, dictionary form, する / くる, longer action sentences, and the first 10 kanji.

Unit 4: full い-adjective and な-adjective systems, negative and past descriptions, adjective+noun phrases, simple comparisons, and a second 10-kanji pack.

Vocabulary mastery, kana mastery, kanji recognition/recall/tracing, adaptive review, lesson checks, and required unit finals are included.
